---
name: Comparison grammar precedence
description: Prevents broad prompt clauses from overriding the actual products or vendors being compared.
---

Treat vendor extraction as a precedence problem, not a collection of phrase-specific exceptions. Explicit `between` pairs outrank earlier descriptive lists; explicit chosen or comma-separated lists, including chained `A vs B vs C` lists, outrank incidental pair matches; other clear comparison pairs outrank broad prepositional clauses. When one prompt contains multiple explicit comparison scopes, prefer the most complete explicit chain and use the later chain to break equal-length ties so a detailed decision context cannot be reduced to an introductory pair. “Models from A and B available…” is an explicit manufacturer list, not a generic comparison pair. Natural option lists may use `/`, commas, repeated `and` or `or`, `vs`, `versus`, or `against`, but clauses beginning with “compare on,” “compare by,” or “compare based on” describe criteria and must never become options.

**Why:** Broad words such as “across” and “from” can introduce either real vendor lists or business-objective text. Giving every match equal priority can put objective fragments into scorecards and recommendation headlines even when research identifies the correct products. Stopping at the first comparison clause can also silently discard a later, more specific decision scope.

**How to apply:** When adding prompt grammar, test it against pair comparisons, chosen lists, comma-, slash-, and conjunction-separated provider lists, prompts with conflicting introductory and decision-context scopes, migration wording, and criteria clauses that contain “and.” Exclude criteria-only follow-up sentences before choosing the largest comparison chain, including “Compare performance, reliability, safety features and maintenance” without an “on” prefix. Assertions must verify the complete final vendor labels, not only whether the prompt is accepted.
